\begin{center}\textbf{Abstract}\end{center}
\small
Mixture-of-Agents pipelines send a task to several proposer models and
have an aggregator write the final answer. We study a much smaller
relative of that design on local hardware: one 20B model writes the
answer from three contributions that, in most experiments, come from
the same model under one-sentence role prompts. Across 67 numbered
experiments registered before they ran, the last six after an internal
review, four groups of results hold. (1)~An instruction to label
estimates with a named phrase produces the phrase in two thirds of
answers (378 runs), and a synonym chosen the day before behaves the
same. (2)~Two local LLM judges prefer the pipeline's answer to a single
answer from the same model (all-pairs scores $0.61$ and $0.58$) and
mark down the instructed phrase; an attempt to add judges from other
model families failed, because three of four chose the first-listed
answer in at least 98\% of judgments. (3)~On 61 questions with computed
answers, the aggregator's answer follows a figure stated in the
question over a contribution that misquotes it ($0$ of $122$ runs
against $112$ of $122$ when the contribution is the only source) and
does not mention the misquote; when two contributions give different
figures and the question gives none, the answer follows the one listed
first about three times in four. (4)~Six reader models from five
developers change their decision when a decisive sentence is appended to
a report ($+0.52$ pooled over four new readers, 11 items), without
being told to read attachments, and with a loss of about $0.12$ when it
is one line among ten. A design assembled from these observations
showed no detectable difference in judge preference from the plain
pipeline at about three and a half times the model calls; in its live
chain, scored a second time after a tool fault was repaired, a second
source raised right answers from $2$ to $19$ of $61$ and the conflict
appeared in $2$ of $61$ final answers. Two questions
stayed unanswered: the sentence-level judge used throughout agrees
little beyond chance and could not be compared with a person, and a
test of whether caveats are conveyed less often than other sentences
was blocked when its judges failed one of four checks. No person
labelled, judged or read anything. We list the corrections, the
amendments and the limits.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Pipelines that combine several language-model outputs into one answer
are widely used. Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) \cite{wang2024moa} reports
that a stack of six open proposer models and an aggregator outperforms
GPT-4o on AlpacaEval~2.0, and attributes the gain to
``collaborativeness'': a model shown other models' drafts writes a
better response. In advisory settings (healthcare, legal and financial
analysis) a common variant gives the proposers specialist roles, on the
expectation that specialists contribute knowledge and caution a single
generalist would lack.

This project began with one question: does such a council of
specialists answer better than one model? At the scale we could run,
the answer was no on every measure available to us. On a 60-item
battery with checkable answers a single 20B model answered $57$ of $59$
items correctly and a specialist-plus-aggregator pipeline $56$ of $60$;
two harder batteries stopped at a ceiling
(Section~\ref{sec:smaller}). The work then turned to narrower questions
about what the aggregation step does with what it is given:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item Does an instruction to the aggregator change what it does, or
only the words it uses?
\item What do LLM preference judges reward in the aggregated answer?
\item What happens to a figure when one contribution, or two
contributions, supply it?
\item How much of the proposers' qualification reaches the answer?
\item Can a follow-up question to a proposer, or a list of caveats
appended to the report, change the outcome?
\end{enumerate}

Prior work has answered parts of these at larger scale. Aggregation
pipelines have been evaluated on accuracy as well as preference
\cite{wang2024moa,li2025selfmoa}, a single deceptive agent has been
shown to damage MoA \cite{wolf2025doge}, models are known to adopt
supplied content over a correct prior \cite{wu2024clasheval,
xie2024chameleon}, LLM judges are known to penalize expressions of
uncertainty \cite{lee2025judges}, and summarization is known to drop
scope-limiting detail \cite{peters2025generalization,belem2026may}.
What this study adds is small-scale, registered, component-level
measurement on one pipeline, together with a record of what went wrong
in measuring it.

\textbf{What this draft claims.} Statements scoped to one 20B model,
in-house scenarios and items, and local LLM judges and readers: an
instruction produces the phrase it names (Section~\ref{sec:phrase}); two
local judges prefer the aggregated answer and mark down the instructed
phrase (Section~\ref{sec:pref}); the aggregator's answer follows a
figure stated in the question over a contribution that misquotes it,
and with two conflicting contributions it follows the one listed first
about three times in four (Sections~\ref{sec:select}
and~\ref{sec:checkable}); and reader models act on a decisive appended
sentence (Section~\ref{sec:res-channel}). We also describe a design
assembled from these observations (Section~\ref{sec:harness}) and
report that it showed no difference from the plain pipeline in judge
preference, and that in its live chain a second source got the right
figure used more often without reliably displacing the wrong one and
without the conflict reaching the final answer
(Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}).

\textbf{What changed from v0.2.} An internal review found errors in the
earlier draft that the repository itself exposes. They are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:corrections}. The three with the largest effect: the
reported share of caveats ``carried through prose'' ($0.174$) came from
a parsing error and is $0.030$ under the paper's own rule; the slope
relating caveats in to caveats out was reported with run-level
intervals and is close to the ratio of answer length to input length;
and the preference shares counted only the minority of pairs each judge
decided consistently. The earlier title and abstract described the
assembled design as a method that ``hardens'' MoA. The evidence does
not support that description, and it has been removed.

\textbf{What changed from v0.3.} Draft v0.3 announced six registered
experiments. They ran between 1 and 5 October 2026 and are reported
here; Section~\ref{sec:planned} gives each one's outcome by its
registered rules. Two of them needed labels from the author that could
not be provided and were amended before any of their results was read.
One was scored a second time after a fault in a text-matching check was
repaired. One result is worded more narrowly than its registration
allowed. All four changes are in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}, and
Appendix~\ref{app:v03} lists the statements of v0.3 that this draft
alters.

\section{Setup and Methods}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{The system as tested.} The aggregator is
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b}, a 20B reasoning model served locally, with the
one-sentence prompt ``You are the lead analyst. Several specialists
have written the contributions below. Write the final answer to the
decision-maker's question.'' Three proposers supply contributions. In
the early experiments (numbered below 30, scored with a keyword
counter that the project later abandoned) the proposers were separate
fine-tuned 7 to 8B domain models. In the experiments this paper
reports, the proposers are \emph{the aggregator model itself} under
prompts of the form ``You are a healthcare analyst advising a
decision-maker\ldots'', one each for healthcare, legal and finance; in
several experiments one stored contribution per role per scenario is
reused across runs. Fine-tuned models appear only in the comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:smaller} and the screening test of
Section~\ref{sec:res-gate}. The pipeline is therefore a role-prompted
self-ensemble with one aggregation layer. It is not MoA as published,
which uses six different proposers of 70B and above over several
layers, and results here should not be read as results about that
system.

\textbf{Comparisons.} The \emph{plain pipeline} is three role-prompted
contributions and the aggregator prompt above. The \emph{single answer}
is the same model answering the scenario with the same prompt minus the
sentence that mentions contributions. The pipeline costs four model
calls and the single answer one. No comparison of equal cost
(for example several samples of the single model with a vote
\cite{wang2023selfconsistency,li2024moreagents}) was run.

\textbf{Scenarios and items.} Eighteen advisory scenarios were written
for the project, spanning healthcare, legal and finance content.
Experiments use 6 to 18 of them. Three item sets were written for
specific tests: 6 planted disagreements, 8 planted figures and 11
decision questions. A fourth set was built by script for experiments 63
and 67: 61 arithmetic and rule questions over 14 templates whose
answers a generator computes, each with one input figure that can be
raised or lowered so that the computed answer changes. Scenarios,
items, planted facts and the labels the project notebook calls
analyst-authored were produced with an AI assistant (see the statement
before the references). No public benchmark is used to evaluate the
pipeline. Two public datasets with human labels are used only to check
judges: FRANK \cite{pagnoni2021frank} and the SFU Review Corpus
annotated for speculation \cite{konstantinova2012sfu}.

\textbf{Registration.} Each experiment has a written entry made before
it ran, with predictions, the result that would count as failure, and a
computation from existing runs of whether the outcome could occur
often enough to measure. There are 65 such entries for the 68 numbered
experiments (experiment 68 is a design that has not run). The entries are in the project's version history ahead of
the corresponding results. They are self-registered, and the run
records for experiments 25 to 60 were added to the history weeks after
their results and carry no timestamps, so the order of registration and
data collection cannot be verified from the records. Records made since
October 2026 carry time, model digest, seed and settings. The
registrations of experiments 62 to 67 were committed before any of
their runs and pushed to the public repository 70 minutes before the
first run of experiment 63; for experiment 62 the push came 50 minutes
after its judge calls had begun. Deviations from the registrations are
listed in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}.

\textbf{Instruments.} \emph{Exact phrase}: containment of an instructed
phrase. In 2{,}907 text spans from runs without any instruction, no
phrase from the project's registry of instructed phrases occurred.
\emph{Sentence labels}: two judges (\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} and
\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}) label each sentence, in batches of ten, for three
kinds of qualification (a number labelled as an assumption; different
jurisdictions treated separately; a stated condition under which the
claim changes), with definitions that name no wording. A sentence
counts when both judges flag it. Raw agreement is $0.83$ to $0.91$, but
most sentences carry no qualification: agreement expected by chance is
$0.64$ to $0.88$ and Cohen's kappa \cite{cohen1960kappa} is $0.19$ to
$0.41$ (Appendix~\ref{app:instruments}). The labels have not been
compared with human labels: experiment 62 was registered to do that and
could not, because the author was unable to provide the labels
(Section~\ref{sec:planned}). Every count that depends on them is
provisional and marked~$\dagger$. \emph{Preference}: a judge model sees
the question and two answers and names the better one; every pair is
judged in both orders. \emph{Exact match} on planted values, after
normalisation of number formats. \emph{Reader decision}: a one-word
PROCEED or HOLD answer from a reader model. \emph{Computed answer}
(experiments 63 and 67): the number on the answer's final line is
compared with the answers a generator script computes under the right
and under the altered input. \emph{Figure present}: whether a figure's
digits occur in a text. As registered, this check does not match a
figure typeset with LaTeX thousands separators, which decided one
registered check by two items (Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}).

\textbf{Preference score.} For a pair judged in both orders let $s=1$
if the first-named side wins both, $s=0$ if it loses both and
$s=\tfrac12$ if the judge's choice follows position. The all-pairs
score of a comparison is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{pref} = \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P} s_p ,
\label{eq:pref}
\end{equation}
over all pairs $P$ with two parsed judgments. The judges used here
choose the first-listed answer in $0.80$ to $0.89$ of single
judgments, so most pairs score $\tfrac12$. The earlier draft reported
the share of wins among the pairs with $s\in\{0,1\}$ only, which
discards 63 to 82\% of pairs and overstates differences.

\textbf{Statistics.} The unit of analysis is the scenario or the item.
For each comparison we average within the unit, take paired
differences, and report the mean, a 95\% $t$-interval and the $p$-value
of an exact sign-flip test, with the number of units $k$. Percentile
bootstrap intervals over 6 to 18 clusters, used in the earlier draft,
are too narrow \cite{cameron2008bootstrap,miller2024errorbars}. A few
intervals are quoted as originally computed and are marked ``as first
reported''. Table~\ref{tab:claims} lists the five statements we treat
as the paper's main claims, with Holm-adjusted $p$-values
\cite{holm1979}; they were chosen after the results were known, so the
adjustment describes rather than protects. The primary predictions of
experiments 62 to 67 were named before the runs;
Table~\ref{tab:planned} gives their outcomes by the registered rules.
Everything else is descriptive.

\section{What the Plain Pipeline Does}
\label{sec:failures}

\subsection{An Instruction Produces the Phrase It Names}

A measurement of an instructed behavior can count compliance with the
instruction instead of the behavior. If the instrument detects strings
the prompt dictated, the measured quantity $M$ is the sum of compliance
$C$ and behavior $B$,
\begin{equation}
M = C + B ,
\label{eq:entangle}
\end{equation}
and separating them needs a design in which the dictated string varies
while everything else is held fixed. Three arms were run on 18
scenarios with 7 repeats each (378 runs): the aggregator prompt alone
as control, and two arms adding the sentence ``When you state a number
that is an estimate rather than an established fact, label it using the
phrase `modeled at'\,'' (form X) or the same sentence with ``taken to
be'' (form Y). Form X had appeared in the project's prompts for months.
Form Y was chosen the day before and appears nowhere in the project's
earlier outputs.

Each instructed arm produced its own phrase in about two thirds of
answers: $+0.667$ \ci{+0.450}{+0.883} for form X and $+0.698$
\ci{+0.504}{+0.893} for form Y over control, which produced neither
(Figure~\ref{fig:swap}, left). Neither arm produced the other's phrase
in 252 runs. A phrase used for months and one chosen the day before
behaved alike.

The share of answers containing an assumption-labelled sentence in
\emph{other} wording was $0.341$ in control, $0.365$ under form X
(difference $+0.024$ \ci{-0.080}{+0.128}, $p=0.75$) and $0.317$ under
form Y ($-0.024$ \ci{-0.131}{+0.083}, $p=0.81$)$^\dagger$. Answer
length was flat across arms. This second result is weaker than the
earlier draft said. It is measured with the two-judge labels, which
flagged $73$ of $110$ sentences containing ``modeled at'' and $35$ of
$101$ containing ``taken to be''. An instrument that misses a third to
two thirds of the instructed phrases could miss an increase expressed
in new wording. The supported statement is that the judges saw no
change larger than about $0.13$; the experiment was registered to
detect $0.22$ and cannot support a claim of equivalence.

Three comparisons were judged on the 18 scenarios, 126 pairs each, in
both orders. Judge 1 is \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}, which wrote both sides of
every pair. Judge 2 is a 30B model from another vendor, chosen from
three candidates on whether it returned parseable and consistent
judgments, without looking at which side it favored.

\emph{Pipeline answer against single answer.} Judge 1 gave 36 wins, 8
losses and 82 position-driven splits, an all-pairs score of $0.611$
($+0.111$ \ci{+0.065}{+0.157} from $0.5$, $p=0.0005$). Judge 2 gave
26, 7 and 93: $0.575$ ($+0.075$ \ci{+0.023}{+0.128}, $p=0.012$). The
two sides' answers differ in length by $1.4\%$, and restricting to
pairs of near-equal length leaves judge 1's share of consistently
decided pairs unchanged ($0.815$ against $0.818$). Which property of the
pipeline answer the judges respond to is not identified.

\emph{Instructed phrase against control.} The arms of
Section~\ref{sec:phrase} differ from control only in the instruction.
Judge 1 scored the ``modeled at'' answers $0.413$ ($-0.087$
\ci{-0.144}{-0.031}, $p=0.009$) and the ``taken to be'' answers $0.440$
($-0.058$ \ci{-0.118}{+0.003}, $p=0.06$). Judge 2 scored them $0.448$
($p=0.17$) and $0.448$ ($-0.052$ \ci{-0.092}{-0.012}, $p=0.023$). Two
of the four comparisons reach $p<0.05$ and all four lean the same way
(Figure~\ref{fig:pref}). The registration had predicted the opposite.

This agrees with larger studies: LLM judges including GPT-4o are biased
against epistemic markers \cite{lee2025judges}, and human preference
data used in alignment are biased against text that expresses
uncertainty \cite{zhou2024relying}. Our contribution is a small
replication with a counterbalanced phrase on local judges. Read with
Section~\ref{sec:phrase}, it suggests a tension for systems tuned
toward such judges: the instruction adds a phrase, and the judges
prefer the answer without it.

\emph{Judges from other families.} Experiment 65 gave the same 378
pairs to four more local judges, under a rule fixed in advance: a judge
counts only if it chooses the first-listed answer in fewer than 95\% of
its single judgments. Two judges of 7 and 8B chose the first-listed
answer in all 756 of their judgments, and a third 7B judge in 740. Only
\texttt{phi4:14b} counted (first-listed in $0.886$), the registered
test needed two, and its three predictions are not evaluable. Alone,
that judge scored the pipeline answer $0.560$ ($+0.060$
\ci{+0.005}{+0.114}, $p=0.050$; 25 wins, 10 losses, 91 splits) and
showed nothing on the two phrase comparisons. On the pairs that it and
one of the two earlier judges both decided, they chose the same side in
25 of 29 and 22 of 27. The preference therefore still rests on two
judges, one of which wrote the answers. Among the pairs each judge
decided, the longer answer won 100 of 131 (judge 1), 70 of 85 (judge 2)
and 42 of 70 (\texttt{phi4}), and the pipeline answer is the longer one
in 72 of 126 pairs; this was computed after the data were seen, and the
restriction to near-equal lengths above left judge 1's result
unchanged.

Three limits apply. The pipeline answer costs four calls and the single
answer one; sampling one model several times and aggregating is known
to help \cite{li2025selfmoa,li2024moreagents}, so the role structure
has not been shown to add anything. The judges are local models whose
single judgments mostly follow position, and at 7 to 8B follow it
entirely, so a pool of judges at this scale cannot widen the evidence.
And no person judged a pair.

Eight scenarios each received one added figure (a price, a rate, a
budget) that appears nowhere in the scenario. One proposer's text
states a value for it, and the question asks for that figure. In the
second arm another proposer states a different value. Answers are
scored by exact match.

With one source, the aggregator stated the planted value in $36$ of
$40$ runs. With two, it stated the planted value in $18$ of $40$ and
the second value in $20$ of $40$. At the item level the drop is
$+0.450$ \ci{+0.095}{+0.805} ($p=0.031$, 8 items) and the rise in the
second value $+0.500$ \ci{+0.090}{+0.910} ($p=0.063$). The change is
all-or-nothing by item (Table~\ref{tab:items}), and two attempts to
predict which source wins, from the model's own plausibility rating and
from an elicited map of which role ``owns'' each quantity, were not
evaluable at their sample sizes.

Three points limit the reading. First, repeating the single value is
compliance with the question; nothing in the scenario allowed the value
to be checked, so the $0.90$ rate is not evidence of a failure to
verify. That models adopt supplied content readily is established
\cite{wu2024clasheval,xie2024chameleon}. Second, with two sources the
aggregator did not report that two values existed; it stated one. A
second source therefore changes the answer in about half of runs
without the reader being told there was a choice. Third, when the same
design was run with a live planning step and live proposers
(experiment 59), the second value was used ($2/40$ against $19/40$,
$+0.425$ \ci{+0.285}{+0.565}, $p=0.008$) but the drop in the planted
value was not established ($25/40$ against $18/40$, $+0.175$
\ci{-0.069}{+0.419}, $p=0.22$). The earlier draft described this
design as ``engineered redundancy'' that ``halves corrupted-value
adoption'' and ``is causal''. Those descriptions are withdrawn. A
figure from an early experiment, $0/27$ planted values adopted when a
clean source remained, is also withdrawn: in that condition 23 of 27
answers stated neither value, and on several items the planted numbers
contradicted the scenario text, which the aggregator followed instead.
Section~\ref{sec:checkable} repeats the design with a figure the
aggregator could check and a scored answer.

Experiment 63 replaces the planted figure with one that matters to a
computed answer. Each of 61 arithmetic and rule questions has one input
figure that, raised or lowered by 20 to 25\%, changes the answer a
generator script computes. The aggregator, with its usual prompt,
receives the question and three one-sentence contributions; a
contribution that supplies a figure quotes the sentence of the question
that contains it. Five layouts differ only in where the right and the
wrong figure sit (Table~\ref{tab:checkable}). Nothing asks the
aggregator to check or to report anything. Each layout was run twice
per question; where two contributions carry a figure, the second run
swaps their order. The final number was scored by script.

\emph{When the question states the figure.} With a contribution
misquoting a figure that the question itself states, the answer
followed the misquote in $0$ of $122$ runs, against $112$ of $122$ when
the contribution was the only source: by template $+0.923$
\ci{+0.840}{+1.005}, exact $p=0.0001$, the smallest value 14 templates
allow. This was the registered primary prediction. No reply mentioned
the misquote, and the model's output, hidden reasoning included, was no
longer than in the layout without a misquote (median $1{,}006$ against
$978$ tokens). The supported statement is that the answer follows the
question's figure. The registration would have allowed ``catches a
wrong figure''. We do not use that wording: an aggregator that ignored
the contributions entirely would produce the same row. The $112$ of
$122$ is close to guaranteed by the layout, in which the question says
the figure is given in the contributions.

\emph{When two contributions conflict.} With the figure removed from
the question and two contributions quoting different figures, one right
and one wrong, the answer was right in $52$ of $122$ runs and followed
the wrong figure in $62$, down from $112$ with one source ($+0.416$
\ci{+0.279}{+0.553}, $p=0.0002$). Nothing in this layout lets the
aggregator tell which figure is right, and the split by listing order
shows what it does: the right figure was followed in $42$ of $61$ runs
when its contribution was listed first and in $10$ of $61$ when it was
listed second. Over the two conflict layouts the answer followed the
first-listed contribution in $170$ of $244$ runs and the second in
$56$; among runs following either, the first-listed share by template
is $0.745$ \ci{0.664}{0.826}. The drop from one source to two is what
choosing one of two produces, and a second source helps only in that
the right figure gets a chance of being chosen. The order statistics
were computed after the data were seen, and the first-listed
contribution is always labelled ``Analyst A'', so position and label
cannot be separated.

\emph{Whether the reader is told.} Both conflicting figures appeared in
the reply in $41$ of the $244$ conflict runs ($0.186$
\ci{0.087}{0.285} by template; registered as below one half). The AI
assistant read 60 of these replies to check the count: three of 40
replies without both figures still said that two figures were on offer,
and one of 20 with both was a coincidence of digits, which puts the
share that let a reader see the conflict near one in five. When the
conflict was acknowledged, the stated reason for the choice was the
order of mention, caution (``to be conservative''), or an unsupported
judgment that one figure was more common, more realistic or a typo.

\emph{Two other aggregator models.} \texttt{phi4:14b} and the 30B model
used as judge 2 ran the same 610 prompts with a 4{,}096-token
allowance. Neither followed the misquote ($1$ and $0$ of $122$). With
conflicting contributions they behaved differently from the main model
and from each other. \texttt{phi4} showed both figures in $236$ of
$244$ replies and in $62$ of them gave an answer for each; the parser,
written for single answers, scored $32$ of those by their last answer
line. The 30B model showed both figures in $164$ replies, and $148$ of
its replies ran to the end of the allowance without a final number.
Where one contribution
was followed, it was the first-listed one in $0.79$ and $0.74$ of runs.
Whether a reader learns of a conflict thus depended on the aggregator
model. That the first-listed contribution usually wins did not.

Let $s$ be the number of caveat sentences in the proposers' text and
$y$ the number in the answer, both counted with the two-judge
labels$^\dagger$. Within a scenario set they are related by
\begin{equation}
y = w\,s + c .
\label{eq:transport}
\end{equation}
For \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} the slope $w$ is $0.172$ and $0.326$ in the
two scenario sets, both with scenario-level intervals above zero
(Figure~\ref{fig:transport}). For a second aggregator model,
\texttt{phi4:14b}, it is $0.105$ and $0.170$, and neither interval
excludes zero. The earlier draft reported intervals that resampled
runs, under which the second model's slope on the newer scenarios
appeared to exclude zero; the claim that the slope ``replicates for a
second writer'' is withdrawn.

The earlier draft also read $w$ as a loss: ``prose transports $0.11$ to
$0.33$ of supplied qualification.'' Table~\ref{tab:shares} shows what
the slope mostly reflects. The answer is 18 to 30\% as long as its
inputs and contains 18 to 31\% as many caveat sentences. The share of
sentences that carry a caveat is about the same in the answer as in the
inputs. A summary is shorter than its sources, and on this evidence
caveats are kept roughly in proportion.

Whether a caveat is less likely to be conveyed than an ordinary
sentence was the subject of experiment 66. Each of 285 caveat
sentences$^\dagger$ was matched with an ordinary sentence of similar
length from the same proposer text, and each sentence was rated
against three stored answers ($1{,}710$ sentence--answer pairs): two
judges said whether its content is conveyed, fully, partly or not, in
the three answer sentences closest to it. The registration required the judges to be checked against 120
pairs labelled blind by the author before the comparison was read.
Those labels could not be provided. Before any judge answer was read,
the check was replaced by four others with bars fixed in advance
(Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}): agreement with human labels on 300
summary sentences from the FRANK benchmark \cite{pagnoni2021frank}, and
three sets of items with a known answer built from stored material the
experiment does not use. The judges passed three. Against the human
labels the two-judge rule reached kappa $0.742$ and F1 $0.906$ (bars
$0.60$ and $0.80$); all 50 sentences copied from an answer were rated
fully conveyed; and 2 of 50 statements from a different scenario were
rated conveyed. They failed the fourth: when a figure was altered in a
statement both judges had rated fully conveyed, they still rated it
fully conveyed in 5 of 25 cases, against a bar of one in ten. One of
the five was a malformed test item and one a statement whose figure was
never in the passages; no way of counting reaches the bar.

By the registered rule the comparison is therefore not evaluable. For
the record it came out even: $0.696$ of caveat sentences and $0.696$ of
ordinary sentences were rated conveyed at least partly ($0.270$ and
$0.260$ fully), a scenario-level difference of $+0.004$
\ci{-0.128}{+0.135} over the 14 scenarios with at least five caveats.
That interval is wider than the $\pm0.10$ band registered for calling
the two rates equal, so the comparison would not have been evaluable
with a passing check either. A judge given the whole answer found the
content of about half of the sentences that the three-sentence check
had rated absent (13 of 30 caveats, 17 of 30 ordinary sentences).
Nothing here suggests that caveats are lost more than other content,
and nothing establishes that they are not. Published work finds that
summarization and rewriting do lose scope and certainty information
\cite{peters2025generalization,belem2026may,wang2026facts}, so the
question remains open here.

On inputs from which every flagged caveat sentence had been removed and
the removal confirmed, the aggregator still wrote at least one caveat
sentence in $4$ of $12$ runs$^\dagger$.

\subsection{A Follow-Up Reply Is Used When It Is Informative}
\label{sec:followup}

The aggregator was run in two stages: it first wrote a required list of
disagreements among the contributions, then the answer. Six items were
built in which two proposers take conflicting positions and a fact
neither stated settles the question. Between the stages one of three
things was added: nothing; a reply in the right style that restates
the proposer's position without the fact; or the same reply with the
fact. Two judge models classified which side each answer took (their
agreement was $0.70$ on the runs analysed and $0.58$ overall).

In the runs where the aggregator had named the planted disagreement,
it took the fact's side in $7$ of $7$ runs with the informative reply,
$5$ of $15$ with no reply and $3$ of $15$ with the uninformative reply.
That contrast conditions on naming, which happened in $7$, $15$ and
$15$ of $36$ runs although the first stage is identical across arms.
Over all $36$ runs per arm the counts are $15$, $10$ and $8$. At the
item level, informative against none is $+0.700$ \ci{+0.145}{+1.255}
among named runs (5 items) and $+0.139$ \ci{+0.007}{+0.271} over all
runs (6 items), with exact $p=0.125$ in both cases; no item goes the
other way. With five or six items no contrast here could be strong.
We report it as a consistent direction: when the fact arrives, the
aggregator uses it. Naming was the scarce step, at $0.21$ to $0.58$ of
runs across three experiments.

\subsection{Smaller Observations}
\label{sec:smaller}

\textbf{Accuracy.} On a 60-item battery with checkable answers the
single model answered $57$ of $59$ items correctly and a pipeline of
the matching fine-tuned specialist plus the aggregator $56$ of $60$
(paired difference \ci{-0.085}{+0.034}, as first reported). Two further
batteries with script-computed answers (36
multi-step computations, 30 rule-interaction items) put the single
model at $0.977$ and $0.983$, and were stopped before any pipeline run
because there was no headroom. We have no evidence that the pipeline
gives more correct answers than one model; on the one battery where
both ran, the difference was indistinguishable from zero and nominally
against the pipeline.

\textbf{Fine-tuned proposers.} Two medical fine-tunes of one base model
shifted in-domain framework vocabulary by $+0.232$
\ci{+0.072}{+0.412} and $+0.109$ \ci{-0.056}{+0.300} over the base (as
first reported). The intervals overlap almost entirely, so the data do
not show that the two differ, as the earlier draft claimed. Stronger
evidence for the practical point exists: medical adaptations outperform
their base models in $12.1\%$ of comparisons and are worse in $38.2\%$
\cite{jeong2024medical}.

\textbf{Self-assessment.} Proposers given a way to defer named the
right colleague in $10$ of $11$ deferrals, and deferred in $0.306$ of
runs outside their field against $0.083$ inside it; the six-item
interval for the difference spans zero. A model's plausibility rating
predicted which value it would state at $0.544$, recorded as not
evaluable, and an elicited map of which role owns each quantity at
$0.500$ \ci{0.323}{0.739} on 28 runs, recorded as a failed prediction;
both tests could detect only a value of $0.68$ or more. The earlier
draft summarized these as self-assessment that ``predicts behavior at
chance''. Three underpowered tests do not support that, and the
project's own rule forbids citing an uninformative null as evidence of
absence. The question is open; a pre-registered study of small open
models finds verbal confidence uninformative for a different reason,
saturation at the ceiling \cite{cacioli2026verbal}.

\textbf{Agreement between proposers.} The earlier draft said that
claims made by two or more proposers reach the answer no more often
than claims made by one. That rested on one of four kinds of
qualification in an analysis made with the abandoned keyword counter;
the other three kinds showed large positive effects of agreement. The
claim is withdrawn. Published work finds that models follow the
majority of the evidence they are given \cite{xie2024chameleon}.

\textbf{Showing proposers each other's text.} With stored sibling
contributions made visible, off-domain vocabulary moved by $-0.009$
\ci{-0.048}{+0.028} (as first reported, 18 scenarios), against a
registered threshold of $+0.05$. Keeping proposers apart is therefore a
cost choice: sibling text adds about 10k input characters per proposer.

\section{A Design Assembled from These Observations}
\label{sec:harness}

The observations above suggest a design with five parts
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}, Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). We describe it as a
proposal. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports what has been tested, and
Table~\ref{tab:built} states where the system that was actually
assembled differs from the parts that were tested.

\textbf{Screen proposers.} A candidate model is admitted only if every
answer on a set of screen prompts reaches 800 visible characters. The
earlier draft also listed a probe of domain vocabulary and a format
test; neither was validated, and the format test failed to predict
anything.

\textbf{Assign each key quantity to two proposers.} A planning step
lists the quantities the answer turns on and writes each into at least
two proposers' sub-questions. Given Section~\ref{sec:select}, the
purpose is to create a visible disagreement when the proposers differ.
It does not make the answer immune to a wrong figure: the aggregator
follows one of the two, usually the one listed first
(Section~\ref{sec:checkable}), and when this part was tested in the
live chain the disagreement was listed and not passed on
(Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}).

\textbf{Trigger a follow-up from a required list.} The aggregator
writes a list of disagreements before it writes the answer. Software
reads the list and sends one follow-up question to the first proposer
named. A list the aggregator produces whenever asked ($391$ of $396$
stored runs) is a more reliable trigger than a model's report of its
own uncertainty, whatever that report is worth.

\textbf{Append the caveats.} Caveat sentences are taken from the
proposers' text and attached to the final document as a list the
aggregator does not rewrite. The aggregator receives no instruction

\subsection{Screening}
\label{sec:res-gate}

Ten local models were screened on two prompts, with the pass or fail
recorded before any pipeline run, and each then wrote 18 proposer
contributions. The two that failed the screen are the two with the most
contributions under 800 characters in the pipeline ($17/18$ and
$6/18$; the other eight had 0 or 1 of 18). Under random labelling of
two of ten models that has probability $1/45 = 0.022$. The earlier
draft reported a pooled contrast with an interval that resampled six
scenarios; the unit that matters is the model. The screen and the
outcome are the same property, short output, measured on different
prompts, so this shows that models which answer very briefly on some
prompts do so on others. One model that had passed the screen weeks
earlier failed it when repeated, so a stored pass should not be reused.

\subsection{Planning Step}
\label{sec:res-planner}

Against 41 labels of key quantities written with the AI assistant, the
planning step recalled $0.633$ \ci{0.506}{0.758} (as first reported),
and did better on quantities that were missing from the scenario
($0.813$) than on stated ones ($0.531$). Told which quantity mattered,
it wrote that quantity into at least two sub-questions in $40$ of $40$
plans, and proposers passed privately supplied values into their
contributions in $0.85$ to $0.95$ of runs. A first attempt stopped at
its registered checkpoint with a useful finding: a proposer conveys a
privately supplied fact only when its sub-question concerns that fact
($2$ of $8$ otherwise). The downstream result is the live-chain rows of
Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\subsection{Appended Caveat List}
\label{sec:res-channel}

\textbf{What the list holds.} The list is built from the proposer
sentences that both judges flag$^\dagger$, so it contains all of them by
construction; the earlier draft reported this ``carriage of $1.000$''
as a result. Across 119 documents, the aggregator's own prose contains $61$ of
the $2{,}037$ listed sentence instances word for word ($0.030$). The earlier draft's $0.174$
came from a parsing error: caveats were read back from the list one
line at a time, and the quarter of them that span several lines were
cut to a first line that is often a single table character. The
corrected figure is a verbatim-copy rate. A caveat restated in the
aggregator's words counts as missing, so it does not measure how many
caveats reach the reader. Experiment 66 attempted that measurement and
its judges failed a check (Section~\ref{sec:transport}); no validated
measure exists.

\textbf{Judge preference.} With the list attached to otherwise
identical documents, judge 1 gave 26 wins, 25 losses and 65 splits. No
penalty was detected and its absence cannot be claimed.

\textbf{A reader model uses a decisive sentence.} Eleven decision
questions were paired with a sentence that should change the decision
and a sentence that should not; some sentences argue for holding and
some for proceeding. A reader model answered PROCEED or HOLD. With the
relevant sentence as the list, reader 1 (\texttt{gpt-oss:20b}) changed
its decision in $0.727$ of reads against $0.036$ for the irrelevant
sentence: $+0.691$ \ci{+0.427}{+0.955}, $p=0.004$
(Figure~\ref{fig:freight}). Placing the same sentence in the prose gave
$0.818$ (prose minus list $+0.091$ \ci{-0.061}{+0.242}). Reader 2
(\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}) gave $+0.455$ \ci{+0.104}{+0.805}, $p=0.063$.

Experiment 64 repeated this with six reader models from five
developers (7 to 30B), four of which experiment 51 had not used, on the
same 11 items ($3{,}300$ reads). Each reader first had to give the
item's default answer when nothing was appended, and all six did.
Pooled over the four new readers, with experiment 51's prompt and a
one-sentence list, the relevant sentence raised the share of changed
decisions by $+0.515$ \ci{+0.277}{+0.753} ($p=0.004$, 11 items) over
the irrelevant one, which sat at the level of no list for every reader.
Removing the clause that told the reader to use anything attached
changed uptake by $-0.017$ \ci{-0.087}{+0.054}; the registered
prediction that the clause mattered failed. Placing the relevant
sentence at a random position among nine real caveat sentences lowered
uptake by $0.118$ \ci{0.031}{0.206} ($p=0.014$), from $0.55$ to $0.43$
pooled over the six readers, with most of the loss in the two smallest
(Table~\ref{tab:readers}). Position in the list made no difference, and
ten real caveats with no relevant sentence did not change decisions
($0.116$ against $0.103$ with no list). The two readers of experiment
51 reproduced their earlier figures in fresh draws. For the smaller
readers uptake is all-or-nothing by item, and two of the 11 items moved
no new reader.

\subsection{Follow-Up with a Live Proposer}
\label{sec:res-loop}

Section~\ref{sec:followup} used replies we wrote. Two experiments used
a live proposer. In the first the deciding fact sat in the proposer's
own first contribution; the aggregator named the disagreement in
$0.21$ of runs and the experiment stopped at its registered checkpoint.
In the second the proposer was given the fact as private notes at reply
time. It conveyed the fact in $21$ of $21$ replies, and the aggregator
took the fact's side in $19$ of those $21$ runs ($23$ of $36$ runs
overall). The earlier draft compared this with the no-reply arm of the
scripted experiment, stored six days earlier ($5/15$; $10/36$). The
aggregator named the disagreement in $0.583$ of the new runs against
$0.417$ of the old ones, so the batches differ, and the item-level
contrast has exact $p=0.125$. We report the counts and claim no effect
size. The proposer was handed the fact, so the result shows that the
route works when the fact exists in the proposer's notes.

In $4$ of the $21$ live replies the proposer added invented legal
authority to the correct fact, and the aggregator used the reply
anyway. A follow-up experiment found invented citations in $0.100$ and
$0.150$ of replies (120 replies; all on one item, where the same two
invented decisions recurred). A blocklist of those names caught every
case in 141 stored replies, which shows only that the filter runs as
written, since its names were taken from those replies.

In 54 runs of the assembled system on the scenarios themselves, with
no planted disagreement, the list named a role and a follow-up went out
in $46$ ($0.85$).

\subsection{Parts That Failed or Were Dropped}
\label{sec:ablations}

A filter meant to drop replies that add nothing failed its registered
criteria twice: a two-judge version dropped $3$ of $6$ uninformative
replies, and a stricter version dropped all six but also rejected
$52\%$ of genuine replies. Replies are delivered with a flag instead.
The ownership map and the plausibility rating
(Section~\ref{sec:smaller}) were not adopted. The rule that proposers
do not see each other's text lost its original rationale
(Section~\ref{sec:smaller}) and stands on cost.

\section{The Assembled System Against the Plain Pipeline}
\label{sec:headtohead}

The assembled system was run three times on each of the 18 scenarios
and its documents were paired with stored answers from the plain
pipeline (108 pairs, both orders, two judges). On all pairs judge 1
scored the assembled system $0.528$ ($+0.031$ \ci{-0.069}{+0.132},
$p=0.57$) and judge 2 scored it $0.477$ ($-0.023$
\ci{-0.060}{+0.014}, $p=0.31$). No difference was detected.

Four things limit this result. No equivalence margin was registered,
so it does not show that the two are equally preferred. Judge 2 decided
only 15 pairs consistently (5 for the assembled system, 10 against), in
9 of 18 scenarios, and its earlier reported interval of
\ci{0.083}{0.562} does not exclude a large loss; the earlier draft's
statement that large effects were excluded in both directions holds for
judge 1 only. The plain pipeline's answers had been stored three weeks
earlier. And the comparison is a bundle: several parts change at once.

\textbf{Cost.} One question takes about fourteen model calls in the
assembled system against four in the plain pipeline, and 14 to 18
minutes on the hardware used. The earlier draft's phrase ``at no cost''
is withdrawn.

\textbf{A wrong figure in the live chain.} Experiment 67 put the 61
questions of Section~\ref{sec:checkable} through the chain with one
model in every role: a planning step, three role-prompted proposers,
the required list of disagreements, one follow-up and the final answer.
The figure was removed from the question and reached the chain only
through a proposer's private notes: the wrong figure in one proposer's
notes and, in the second arm, the right figure in another's. The plan
and the texts of the two proposers whose notes do not change were
generated once and shared between the arms (122 runs, 710 model calls).

The registration required each figure to appear in its proposer's text
in at least $0.75$ of items before the predictions could be read. As
first scored, the wrong figure appeared in $44$ of $61$ texts and the
check failed. Both missing texts contain the figure typeset with LaTeX
separators, which the registered text-matching check does not
recognise; the fault had been recorded three days earlier, in the
reading of experiment 63, and not repaired. At the author's decision
the experiment was scored again with that one repair, giving $46$ of
$61$ and, for the right figure, $51$ of $61$. Both scorings are on
record (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). The check then passes by one
item, and what follows should be read with that history.

With a second source, right final answers rose from $2$ of $61$ to
$19$ of $61$ ($+0.290$ \ci{+0.188}{+0.393}, $p=0.0002$, 14 templates).
The share following the wrong figure fell from $31$ to $24$ of $61$
($+0.130$ \ci{+0.010}{+0.250}); the exact test gives $p=0.055$, so the
registered primary prediction, which required $p<0.05$, was not met.
This is the pattern of experiment 59 on 61 scored items: the second
source's figure is used, and the fall in the wrong figure is not
established. The final answer showed both figures in $2$ of $61$ runs,
although the aggregator's own list of disagreements named both in $31$.
The step meant to expose a disagreement recorded it and did not pass it
on.

Three further observations were not registered tests. Of the $43$
answers that followed one of the two proposers, $35$ followed the one
whose contribution was listed first: the list of disagreements names
that proposer first, the follow-up goes there (to the holder of the
wrong figure in $22$ of $33$ runs when it was listed first; to the
holder of the right figure in $16$ of $28$ when that one was), and the
answer keeps that proposer's figure. When the figure reached no
proposer's text ($21$ runs), the chain still returned a number every
time, and in $20$ of them it was neither the right nor the planted
answer. And the live chain showed the conflict in fewer final answers
than the aggregator alone did in Section~\ref{sec:checkable} ($0.03$
against $0.20$).

\textbf{What has not been measured.} The assembled system's effect on
caveats reaching a reader has not been measured as a system, and its
appended list and its screen played no part in experiment 67.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{One model in every role.} Proposers and aggregator are the same
20B model in the experiments that carry the paper's claims. Results may
not hold for different or larger models, or for proposers that differ
from the aggregator.

\textbf{The baseline is not the published method and is not matched for
cost.} See Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\textbf{No human measurement.} Every judge is a local model of 7 to
30B and every reader is a model. No sentence label, preference or
decision comes from a person. Experiments 62 and 66 were registered to
obtain labels from the author; the labels could not be provided.
Public human labels on neighbouring tasks were used instead, as a check
with registered bars for one experiment and as a reported figure for
the other. The items and several label sets were
written with the same AI assistant that designed the experiments, which
is the instrument-independence problem of Equation~\ref{eq:entangle} at
one remove.

\textbf{The sentence instrument is not validated.} Its chance-corrected
agreement is low, it misses a third to two thirds of the instructed
phrases, a judge shown the same sentence in a different batch agrees
with its own earlier label at kappa $0.42$ to $0.71$, and the
registered comparison with a person could not be made
(Appendix~\ref{app:instruments}). Results marked~$\dagger$ depend on it
and stay provisional.

\textbf{The conveyed judge failed a check.} The two-judge rating of
whether a sentence's content reaches the answer agreed with public
human labels and failed on statements with an altered figure. The
comparison of caveats with ordinary sentences is not evaluable
(Section~\ref{sec:transport}).

\textbf{Few units.} Claims rest on 5 to 18 items or scenarios written
in-house, or on 14 templates of scripted questions. Several effects are
all-or-nothing by item. The results on figures that can be checked come
from arithmetic and rule questions with one-sentence contributions, not
from the advisory scenarios.

\textbf{Constructed outcomes.} In several component tests the outcome
was close to guaranteed by the design: a question that asks for the
planted figure; a list scored against the sentences it was built from;
a decisive sentence placed before the question; a proposer handed the
deciding fact; a question that points to the contributions for a figure
only they contain. These tests show that a route works. They do not show
how the pipeline behaves on inputs nobody arranged.

\textbf{Registration.} The registrations are the author's own, in the
author's repository, and until October 2026 the run records could not

registrations amended before their data were read and one experiment

\textbf{Many tests.} About ninety registered predictions were made over

\textbf{Error rate of the record.} The project notebook logs four
errors in code that printed verdicts, three text-matching errors, five
token-budget failures, one illustrative quotation in an early draft

may remain.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Aggregation pipelines.} MoA \cite{wang2024moa} reports
preference-judged results on AlpacaEval, MT-Bench and FLASK and, in an
appendix, accuracy on MATH by layer. Self-MoA \cite{li2025selfmoa}
shows that aggregating repeated samples of the single best model
outperforms mixing different models, on AlpacaEval and on MMLU, CRUX
and MATH, and sampling one model many times with a vote scales with the
number of samples \cite{li2024moreagents,wang2023selfconsistency}.
These are the missing baselines of Section~\ref{sec:pref}. A single
strongly prompted agent can match multi-agent discussion
\cite{wang2024rebuttal}. The earlier draft said that every published
MoA number came from a preference judge; that was wrong.

\textbf{Wrong inputs.} Wolf et al.\ \cite{wolf2025doge} insert one
deceptive agent into a three-layer MoA and measure the damage on
AlpacaEval~2.0 (win rate $49.2\%$ to $37.9\%$) and on QuALITY accuracy,
with voting-style defenses that recover most of it. Models adopt
incorrect supplied content over a correct prior more than $60\%$ of the
time \cite{wu2024clasheval} and are highly receptive to coherent
external evidence \cite{xie2024chameleon}. The earlier draft said no
published evaluation measures whether a corrupted proposer corrupts the
answer; that was also wrong. Sections~\ref{sec:select}
and~\ref{sec:checkable} are small numeric-value variants, with a
second-source control and with a control in which the question itself
states the figure.

\textbf{Judges and uncertainty.} LLM judges including GPT-4o are biased
against epistemic markers \cite{lee2025judges}; human preference data
are biased against text that expresses uncertainty
\cite{zhou2024relying}; judges show position bias that varies by model
\cite{zheng2023judging}, length correlations under preference
optimization \cite{singhal2023length}, and sycophancy
\cite{sharma2023sycophancy}. Our judges' position bias should be read
as a property of the small local judges tested.

\textbf{Loss of qualification.} LLM summaries overgeneralize scientific
findings in 26 to 73\% of cases \cite{peters2025generalization},
rewriting raises expressed certainty more often than it lowers it
\cite{belem2026may}, and handoff summaries between agents keep facts
while weakening the conditions on their use \cite{wang2026facts}.
Section~\ref{sec:transport} attempted a comparable measurement and
could not validate its judges.

\textbf{Specialist models and confidence.} Medical adaptations rarely
outperform their base models \cite{jeong2024medical}. Verbal confidence
in small open models fails a pre-registered validity screen
\cite{cacioli2026verbal}.

\textbf{Registration.} Pre-registration of experiments with AI agents
has a proposed template that lists outcome-contingent redesign among
the main risks \cite{vaccaro2026prereg}; the deviations in
Appendix~\ref{app:deviations} are instances. We are not aware of an
earlier experiment that swaps the phrase named by an instruction under
otherwise identical prompts, or of one that tests an appended caveat
list with a matched irrelevant control.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On one 20B model, eighteen in-house scenarios and a scripted set of 61
questions with computed answers, these behaviors of a role-prompted
aggregation pipeline were observed under registered tests. An
instruction to mark estimates produces the phrase it names. Two local
judges prefer the aggregated answer to a single answer and mark the
instructed phrase down. The aggregator's answer follows a figure stated
in the question over a contribution that misquotes it, without
mentioning the misquote. Given two contributions that conflict, it
follows one of them, usually the first listed, and the model studied
most rarely says there were two. And reader models from five
developers act on a decisive sentence appended to the report.

A design assembled from these observations was indistinguishable from
the plain pipeline on a weak preference test. In its live chain a
second source got the right figure used more often, and the step meant
to expose disagreements listed them without passing them on. Three
questions the review raised could not be answered by one person with
local models: whether the sentence judges match a person, whether
caveats are conveyed less often than other content, and whether the
judges' preference extends beyond two models. Whether any of this helps
a person making a decision is unknown.

The measurement lessons may be the more durable result. An instrument
that counts strings the prompt dictated measures compliance. Raw
agreement between judges says little when one answer dominates.
Intervals over a handful of scenarios need methods built for few
clusters. A text-matching check has to be tried on the writing habits
of the model whose text it will read. Small judges given two long
answers choose the first. And a number that lands inside the expected
range still needs checking.

\section*{Impact Statement}

This work concerns language-model pipelines in advisory settings. Its
findings are limited to 7 to 30B local models, English advisory
scenarios written for the project, and small numbers of items, and they
involve no human participants. They should not be used to justify
deploying such a pipeline for real advice. The observation that an
aggregator given two different figures follows the one listed first,
and that the model studied most does so without flagging the conflict,
is a reason for caution wherever such pipelines are used.

\section{Experimental Details}
\label{app:details}

\textbf{Phrase-swap (41).} 18 scenarios, 7 repeats, 3 arms, 378 runs.
Answers averaged $43.7$ to $46.6$ sentences across arms. A check
refused to run an arm whose prompt contained any registry phrase other
than its own.

\textbf{Preference (43, 43-R).} 126 pairs per comparison, both orders.
Judge 1 \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}; judge 2 a 30B model selected from three
candidates on parse rate and consistency only. Pairs decided
consistently: 44, 46, 41 (judge 1) and 33, 29, 23 (judge 2).
First-listed answer chosen in $0.80$ to $0.83$ (judge 1) and $0.87$ to
$0.89$ (judge 2) of single judgments. Mean answer lengths $7{,}757$ and
$7{,}649$ characters for the pipeline and single answers.

\textbf{Second source (47, 59).} 8 items, 5 repeats, 2 arms. Exact
match on values after number-format normalisation. In experiment 59 the
planning step was told which quantity to cover.

\textbf{Caveats in and out (30, 46).} 144 runs, 18 scenarios, two
aggregator models, two scenario sets. Supply was varied by removing
flagged sentences from proposer text.

\textbf{Follow-up (44, 54, 56).} 6 items, 36 runs per arm. Named runs:
7 (informative), 15 (none), 15 (uninformative); 21 of 36 in the live
experiment. Fabrication follow-up: 120 replies.

\textbf{Appended list (48).} 17 scenarios with at least one flagged
sentence, 7 stored answers each, 119 documents, lists of 1 to 53
sentences (median 11). \textbf{Reader (51).} 18 items written, 11
retained after the pilot check; 2 readers, 4 arms, 5 repeats, 440
reads.

\textbf{Screen (55).} Ten models, two screen prompts, six answers each;
18 pipeline contributions per model.

\textbf{Assembled system (61).} 54 runs; 108 pairs against stored
plain-pipeline answers; lists of about 5 sentences.

\textbf{Checkable figure (63).} 61 questions kept from 66 (one integer
input that occurs once and whose raised and lowered values give three
answers further apart than twice the scoring tolerance), 14 templates,
5 layouts, 2 runs: 610 runs per model, temperature $0.6$.
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} had a 16{,}384-token allowance and the two other
models 4{,}096. No run hit the context limit. Three of the main model's
runs returned an empty reply at first, after spending the whole
allowance on reasoning, and were drawn again under the registered retry
rule; all three are on one template. Its first-layout misses are
arithmetic slips.

\textbf{Readers (64).} 6 readers, 11 items, 10 arms, 5 repeats: 550
reads per reader, temperature $0.8$. Surrounding caveat sentences come
from the item's own scenario where it has enough (four items for the
list of ten) and are topped up from other scenarios elsewhere; the relevant and irrelevant
arms share surroundings and position. One reader (\texttt{phi4:14b})
needed more than one try for a one-word answer in 137 of 550 reads and
gave none in 14; the others answered at the first try.

\textbf{Added judges (65).} 378 pairs, both orders, 4 judges, $3{,}024$
judgments at temperature $0$. Every reply parsed and none hit a context
limit. Pairs decided: 16 (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}), 70 (\texttt{phi4:14b}),
0 and 0.

\textbf{Conveyed (66).} 285 caveat sentences matched to ordinary
sentences by length within the same proposer text; 3 stored answers
each; the three answer sentences closest by embedding shown to the
judges; $3{,}420$ judge calls, all parsed. The two judges agree with
each other on ``conveyed at all'' at kappa $0.572$. Check: 300 FRANK
summary sentences with unanimous labels (150 without error, 150 with a

\textbf{Live chain (67).} 61 items, 2 arms, one run each; roles

The instrument was accepted on raw agreement against a bar of $0.70$,
which independent judges would pass at these base rates. It had been
checked on a sample chosen to be rich in positives, where kappa was
$0.51$ to $0.66$, and on twenty anchor sentences written within the

four. On sentences that are positives by construction, both judges
flag $0.66$ (``modeled at'') and $0.35$ (``taken to be''). An earlier

\textbf{Experiment 62.} A sample of 404 stored sentences was drawn,
stratified so that about one in six carries each kind, and labelled by
six local judges. The registered reference, blind labels from the
author made twice a week apart, could not be provided, so the
instrument remains uncompared with a person. On that sample, kappa
between pairs of judges runs from $0.27$ to $0.71$ with a median near
$0.5$. The deployed pair agrees at $0.54$ to $0.61$, higher than in
Table~\ref{tab:kappa} only because the kinds are several times more
frequent in the stratified sample. Each deployed judge, shown the same
sentence again in a different batch of ten, agrees with its own stored
label at kappa $0.42$ to $0.71$, so part of the disagreement between
judges is a label that moves with the batch. On 400 sentences from the
SFU Review Corpus \cite{konstantinova2012sfu}, where annotators marked
speculation cues (modality such as ``could'' or ``I think'', which is
a neighbouring construct and not the same one), the deployed rule
marked $0.090$ of cue sentences and $0.010$ of cue-free sentences as
hedging; single judges ranged from $0.155$ to $0.470$ and from $0.010$
to $0.190$. None of this is validity.

Other measurement findings recorded by the project and relevant here:
an early judge prompt listed the same seven phrases the writing prompts
dictated, found by a registry of every instructed phrase (125 entries);
an instruction moved the dictated phrasing from $0/30$ to $28/30$
answers while other phrasings of the same idea moved from $1/30$ to
$4/30$; empty judge replies had at one point been scored as negative
labels; pairwise judging of two long answers is decided entirely by
position for the 7 to 8B judges tried (experiment 65); and a check that
looks for a figure's digits misses figures the model typesets, finding
the stated figure in 94 of 122 replies of experiment 63 where 111 hold
it.

\section{Diagnostics That Are Not Optimized}
\label{app:charter}

The design records the following and does not tune toward them.
\emph{How often a disagreement is named or a follow-up sent}: raising
it without new information reproduces the uninformative-reply arm of
Section~\ref{sec:followup}. \emph{Counts of hedges or instructed
phrases}: an instruction raises the phrase
(Section~\ref{sec:phrase}), and the judges mark it down
(Section~\ref{sec:pref}). \emph{The slope $w$}: it tracks answer
length, and tuning it against the aggregator's own text would reward
caveats the aggregator adds itself. \emph{Judge preference on qualified
text}: the judges mostly follow position and mark qualification down.
The quantities worth optimizing are checked outside the pipeline's own
text, such as exact-match correctness on items with known answers.
