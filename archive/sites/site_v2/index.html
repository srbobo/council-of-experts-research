A research story in sixty-one experiments

We tried to prove a panel of AI experts beats one strong model.

We mostly failed — and that failure turned out to be the discovery. The panel isn't smarter. But rebuilt around what the evidence actually supports, it becomes something rarer: an answer you can audit.

Part one · The idea

A council of experts

Imagine bringing a hard decision to three advisers at once — say a clinician, a lawyer, and an accountant. Each studies the problem from their own angle and writes their own analysis. Then a chief of staff reads all three and writes you one clear recommendation.

That's the Council of Experts: several specialist AI models work a question independently, and a lead writer — we call it the editor — merges their work into a single answer. The specialists can come from any industry: clinical, regulatory, financial, engineering, supply chain. The seats are interchangeable; the structure is the point.

This is not "mixture of experts"

If you follow AI, you may have heard of mixture-of-experts models. Those are single neural networks whose internal circuitry is divided into blocks, with traffic routed between them invisibly, millions of times per answer. You can't read what an internal "expert" contributed — there's nothing to read.

The council lives at a different level entirely. Its experts are whole, separate models, and every contribution is a document you can open: three analyses, an editor's draft, a paper trail. That visibility is what makes everything in this story measurable — and, eventually, what the whole project came to be about.

The bet

Our starting hypothesis was the industry's conventional wisdom: specialists together beat a generalist alone — more accurate, more careful, better coverage. And we added a rule that shaped everything after: every claim would be written down before the test, with the conditions that would kill it. If we were wrong, we'd know — and we'd say so.

What follows is what sixty-one experiments did to that bet — told finding by finding, while the thing we ended up building assembles itself alongside.

Part two · What the experiments said

Thirteen findings, one machine

Finding 1

The panel isn't smarter.

We built three separate accuracy showdowns against a single strong model — business calculations, tangled rule puzzles, checkable answers throughout. The single model scored 97–98% every time. There was simply no room for a panel to add correctness.

single model alone: 98% on our hardest tests
Finding 2

A specialist label means nothing.

Two "medical" AI models built from the same base disagreed with each other more than with a generalist. And a screening test we built later genuinely forecast which models would break down on the job — including one whose old passing grade had silently expired.

screen-failers broke down in service; screen-passers ran clean
Finding 3

Telling the editor to be careful only buys the words.

Instruct the editor to flag its assumptions with a particular phrase, and that exact phrase appears — in two-thirds of answers. Swap in a synonym and the new word appears instead. Actual carefulness beneath the wording? Unchanged, every time we measured it.

the phrase: installed · the behavior: unmoved
Finding 4

But the merged answer genuinely reads better.

In blind side-by-side matchups — order flipped both ways so nobody wins by going first — the panel's merged answer beat the same model answering alone 82% of the time. A second, unrelated judge agreed. This is the one thing the original design got right.

panel vs. going solo: 82% of decisive matchups
Finding 5

And the same judges punish visible caution.

Answers carrying instructed cautious phrasing lost to their own plain twins — winning only about a quarter of matchups. Any system tuned to please readers will quietly shed its hedges and disclaimers. Careful language and crowd appeal pull in opposite directions, measurably.

cautious phrasing's win rate: 26–32%
Finding 6

Caution dies in transit anyway.

Even when specialists write careful qualifications, the editor's rewrite destroys most of them: only one to three of every ten survive into the final answer. Measured twice, with two different editors. Prose is simply a lossy channel for the fine print.

qualifications surviving the rewrite: 11–33%
Finding 7

The editor doesn't check facts — it chooses between sources.

Plant a wrong number in one specialist's work and the editor repeats it 90% of the time. Give a second specialist the same figure, correct, and errors are cut in half — the editor actively picks the clean source. Protection isn't judgment; it's coverage. So we made coverage a job: a planner that assigns every key figure to two specialists.

wrong number repeated: 90% alone → 45% with a clean second source
Finding 8

Models don't know what they don't know.

Specialists recognized questions outside their expertise only 30% of the time — one deferred its own specialty away. The editor holds confident opinions about which specialist "owns" which figure; tested on fresh cases, those opinions predicted its choices exactly as well as a coin flip. Self-assessment came up empty everywhere we looked.

the "ownership" theory's prediction rate: 50% — a coin flip
Finding 9

A pointed follow-up works — end to end.

Make the editor list where specialists disagree — a document, not a feeling — and send one follow-up question back to the right specialist. With a real specialist answering from its own knowledge, the editor landed the right resolution 90% of the time versus 33% without the loop. Empty, vibes-only replies made things worse than silence.

right resolution: 90% with the loop, 33% without
Finding 10

Assumptions shipped in their own section actually get used.

Attach the specialists' assumptions as a section of the final document — assembled by machinery, never rewritten by the editor — and 100% arrive, versus 17% through prose. Readers act on them: a decision-relevant caveat flipped a reader's call 73% of the time. And judges don't punish the section; if anything they like it slightly.

assumptions delivered: 100% · reader decisions changed: 73%
Finding 11

Under pressure, specialists invent sources.

Asked about a topic where it lacked the facts, one specialist repeatedly cited the same two court cases — cases that don't exist. Invention ran 10–15% of replies, concentrated on trigger topics, and never once came with an admission of uncertainty. The same fakes recur, so a filter for known fabrications works; our attempt at a vaguer "empty reply" detector failed its own tests twice, and we shipped the honest version: flag, never hide.

the same invented court case, 14 independent times
Finding 12

We were wrong about contamination — and published it.

We kept specialists from seeing each other's work, sure that peeking would drag them out of their lanes. Tested properly: nothing. No drift, at a sensitivity that would have caught a third of the everyday level. The rule survives only because sharing costs computing time. An architecture is trustworthy exactly to the degree it advertises its own overturned beliefs.

lane drift from peeking: none detectable
Finding 13

Head-to-head with the original: the safety comes free.

The rebuilt panel faced the original in a final blind taste test. A draw — same reader appeal, no penalty for all the added machinery. But now every answer carries its assumptions, its error protection, and its fabrication screen. And on real questions, the follow-up loop fired 85% of the time: genuine disagreements are everywhere once you look.

reader appeal: a draw · the audit trail: free

Part three · The pivot

We stopped chasing "smarter" and built "auditable"

Midway through, the honest reading of our own ledger forced a turn. The panel would never win on raw correctness — a strong single model already delivers that on any well-posed question. But three things had survived every attempt to kill them: the merged answer reads better; engineered coverage catches errors; and a pointed follow-up resolves disagreements.

So the second half of the program rebuilt the council around only what the evidence supports — and then attacked every piece of the new design the same way we'd attacked the old one. Two of our own design beliefs died in that process. They're in the record with everything else.

The result is the Harness: not a smarter panel — a panel whose every answer arrives with its homework attached.

Part four · What we built

The Harness

One question in, one audited answer out. Every component below exists because a specific experiment earned it — and each card says how it fared when we tried to break it.

held up under test

Qualification gate

No model is seated for its label. A screening test — which genuinely forecast on-the-job breakdowns, and which must be re-run because passing grades expire.

held up under test

The planner

Finds the figures a decision turns on — best at spotting the ones that are missing — and assigns each to at least two specialists. That coverage is where error protection lives.

kept on cost, not fear

Independent specialists

Each seat works its own brief, in any industry. They don't see each other's drafts — not because peeking corrupts (we tested; it doesn't) but because sharing costs computing time.

held up under test

The follow-up loop

The editor must list where specialists disagree; one pointed question goes back to the right seat. Works end to end with live specialists — and fires constantly on real questions.

one filter shipped, one honestly didn't

Reply filters

Known fabrications are screened out by a growing blocklist. Our detector for empty, say-nothing replies failed its own tests twice — so replies ship flagged, never silently dropped.

held up under test

The assumptions track

Every caveat the specialists state travels outside the editor's rewrite, word for word, into a section of the final answer. All of it arrives; readers act on it; judges don't mind it.

Where it landed

The finished Harness faced the original council in one last blind comparison: a draw on reader appeal, which is exactly the point — the audit trail costs nothing. Sixty-one experiments, every one recorded before it ran, every miss published beside every win.
